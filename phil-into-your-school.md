# Getting Phil into your own school

A route that protects you as well as the school, and leaves you with a reference
worth using.

---

## The thing to get right first

You are about to be two people at once: the school's behaviour lead, and the
owner of a company the school is about to put pupil safeguarding notes into.

That is a normal way for good education software to start. What matters is that
it happens **openly and on paper**, because the same arrangement handled quietly
looks very different in hindsight. If a governor or a parent later asked "who
owns the system holding my child's records, and did the head know?", you want a
dated email, not a memory of a corridor conversation.

Done properly this is also your strongest asset. A school that adopted Phil
after its data protection officer scrutinised it is a far better reference than
one that just started using it.

---

## Step 1 — Tell the head, in writing, before anything else

Not a conversation. An email, so it exists.

Cover four things: that you have built a mentoring platform, that you own it,
that you would like the school to use it free of charge for as long as it is
useful, and that you will not charge them without a separate written agreement.

Ask for two things: permission in principle, and an introduction to whoever
handles data protection.

**Do not set up the account before this email.** The order matters. If the
account exists first, you are asking for approval of something already done.

---

## Step 2 — Declare the interest formally

Most schools have a register of business interests, usually held by the
headteacher's PA or the clerk to governors. Ask to be added to it.

This takes ten minutes and it is the single thing that turns "an employee put
pupil data in his own product" into "a declared interest the school assessed and
accepted".

If the school has no register, write to the head stating the interest and ask
for it to be filed. Keep a copy.

---

## Step 3 — Give the DPO the pack, and let them do their job

Send the data processing agreement, the retention policy and the parent privacy
notice. Say plainly that you would rather they picked holes in it now than after
pupil data is in the system.

Expect questions about where data is stored, who at Phil can see it, what
happens when the school stops using it, and what Phil does when a mentor records
a safeguarding concern. You have answers to all four. The last one is the most
important: **Phil does nothing. It never alerts, never notifies a DSL, never
monitors. The mentor follows the school's normal route the same day.**

Sign the DPA even though it is free. A written agreement protects you more than
it protects them.

Whatever they push back on, fix it and tell them you have. A DPO who has seen
you take a correction is a DPO who will vouch for you.

---

## Step 4 — Complete a DPIA with them

Mentoring records about children, sometimes touching on health and welfare,
usually warrant a data protection impact assessment. The school completes it;
you supply the detail.

Offer this rather than waiting to be asked. It signals that you know what you
are handling.

---

## Step 5 — Set the account up

In the Phil staff console, add the establishment with:

- **Plan:** school
- **Payment method:** none
- **Admin:** ideally *not* you

That gives 15 mentor seats, no pupil cap, full access to every course, and no
renewal date, so nothing expires and the retention check never flags it. It
stays that way until you change it.

**On the admin account:** if there is a deputy head or pastoral lead who could
hold it, give it to them. It separates "the school's system" from "your system"
in a way everybody can see. If that is not practical, hold it yourself and say
so in writing.

---

## Step 6 — Start narrow

One mentor. Two or three pupils. One course you know well.

The temptation is to open it to the whole pastoral team at once. Resist it. A
small start means the first problems are yours to fix quietly, and the first
report you show a colleague is one you have checked.

---

## Step 7 — Tell parents

The school issues the privacy notice, not you. Adapt the parent notice in your
pack to sit alongside the school's own wording.

Do this before the first session, not after. A parent who hears about it from
their child first will ask why they were not told, and they will be right.

---

## Step 8 — Wait for something worth showing

Do not ask for a reference or a testimonial in the first term. Run courses,
finish them, generate the reports, and let the school form a view.

**The moment to ask is when a report is used for something real** — a parents'
evening, a governor's question, a review meeting. That is when a head can say
something specific, and specific is what another school will believe.

# Pricing for multi-academy trusts

Your published pricing covers one school at £750 a year. A trust is a different
buyer with a different question, and you will be asked about it.

## Why trusts need their own price

A trust does not buy fifteen copies of a thing. It buys once, centrally, and
expects a discount for volume and a single invoice. A finance director asked to
raise twelve separate £750 purchase orders will usually say no on the
administration alone.

There is also a real difference in what they want: **trust-level reporting**.
A director of education wants to compare schools, see where mentoring is
happening and where it is not, and answer a trustee's question across the whole
group. That is a feature you do not have yet, and it is the thing a trust would
actually pay a premium for.

## A defensible structure

Priced from your existing £750, with volume discount rising by band:

| Schools in trust | Per school | Trust pays | Discount |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2–4 | £675 | £1,350–£2,700 | 10% |
| 5–9 | £600 | £3,000–£5,400 | 20% |
| 10–19 | £525 | £5,250–£9,975 | 30% |
| 20+ | £450 | £9,000+ | 40% |

**One invoice to the trust, one purchase order, one renewal date.** That is
half the value of the offer.

**Multi-year on top**, using the discounts you already publish: 10% for two
years paid upfront, 15% for three.

## What to say when you cannot yet do trust reporting

Be straight about it. "Each school gets its own reporting today. Trust-level
reporting across schools is on the roadmap and I would want your input on what
it should show."

That is a better answer than implying it exists, and it hands them a reason to
stay involved. A trust that shapes a feature tends to buy it.

## The number to hold in your head

**A ten-school trust at £525 is £5,250 a year from one conversation**, against
£750 from a single school. Trusts are slower and involve more people, but the
arithmetic is why they are worth the patience.

## What not to do

**Do not discount below £450 a school.** Below that you are competing on price
with tools that do far less, and you set a floor you cannot climb back from.

**Do not offer a trust-wide free pilot.** Pilot one or two schools within the
trust. A free pilot across twelve schools is a lot of support work for an
outcome nobody owns.

# On finding school two

You have connections to other schools, which is the right route. Cold outreach
to schools is slow and mostly ignored.

**The best introduction is not "would you look at my product". It is "I have
been running this in my school, here is what came out of it, would it be useful
to you?"** That needs the first school to have run real courses, which is why
step 8 matters.

Worth thinking about, and we can work through it properly when you are ready:

- Local authority behaviour and inclusion networks, which usually meet termly
- Teaching school hubs and their pastoral leads
- SEMH and alternative provision networks, where the need is most acute
- Any school your own school already partners with for transition or outreach

**One school running Phil properly, with a head willing to take a phone call,
is worth more than fifty cold emails.**
